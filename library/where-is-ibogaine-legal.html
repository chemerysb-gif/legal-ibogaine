Where is ibogaine legal?

Most countries fall into one of three categories, and the differences matter more than a map suggests. This entry sets out what prohibition, prescription-only status and absence of regulation each mean for someone seeking treatment.

Countries fall into three patterns rather than onto a spectrum. Ibogaine is prohibited outright in some, classified as a prescription medicine with no approved product in others, and in a third group it is not addressed by law at all. The third category is the one most treatment takes place in, and it is the one people misread most often.

Three patterns, not a list

A country-by-country table invites a particular mistake, which is reading a legal status as a safety rating. The two are close to independent.

Prohibition reflects a drug-control decision, usually taken decades ago and often grouped with hallucinogens generally. Prescription status reflects a medicines-regulation decision. Absence of regulation reflects no decision at all.

None of those three tells a person whether a particular provider screens properly, monitors continuously or has emergency equipment. That is why the legal question and the safety question have to be asked separately.

Where it is prohibited

The United States is the clearest example. Ibogaine is listed in Schedule I of the federal controlled substances schedules, among hallucinogenic substances, under DEA code 7260 [1].

Schedule I is the most restrictive category, reserved for substances treated as having no accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse. Manufacture, distribution and possession are federal offences.

This is enforced rather than nominal. In September 2025 the Drug Enforcement Administration announced a 48-month sentence for ibogaine distribution [7].

Several other countries prohibit it by different routes. Some list it under narcotics legislation, and the United Kingdom covers it through general psychoactive substances legislation rather than by naming it.

The practical consequence is the same wherever prohibition applies. Treatment cannot be obtained lawfully, importing the substance is an offence, and people travel instead.

Prohibition has a second effect that is easy to miss. Where a substance cannot be possessed lawfully, it also cannot easily be studied, supplied in known purity, or discussed with a doctor without risk. Some of the uncertainty in this field is a product of its legal position rather than of the pharmacology, and a drug that is difficult to research stays poorly characterised for longer than its properties alone would require.

Where it is a prescription medicine

New Zealand is the most documented example of the second pattern, and it is more interesting than either prohibition or absence.

Medsafe's Medicines Classification Committee considered ibogaine at its meeting in November 2009 and agreed with the recommendation that ibogaine and its metabolite noribogaine be classified as prescription medicines [2]. Part of the reasoning was to limit self-treatment and recreational use [2].

Prescription classification is not approval. No ibogaine product has been approved for marketing there, so it remains an unapproved medicine that a doctor may nonetheless obtain and administer for a patient under their care, using the exemptions the Medicines Act provides [3].

That distinction is the one most often collapsed. A prescriber may legally use it; that is different from a regulator having assessed a product and approved it for sale.

Australia and Canada sit in broadly the same category, reached by their own routes, with prescription status and no approved product.

Where it is simply unregulated

The third pattern covers countries where ibogaine is not named in drug law and no medicines regulator has classified it.

Mexico is the most significant example in practice, because a large share of treatment sought by people from the United States takes place there. Costa Rica is another.

Brazil was described as unregulated in the published account of a clinic series there, in which ibogaine was combined with psychotherapy under a physician's supervision [5]. That study is one of the few published records of what treatment in such a setting involved.

Absence of regulation is why the sector exists in the form it does. A provider in an unregulated country faces no licensing requirement specific to ibogaine, no mandated screening protocol and no required monitoring standard.

Unregulated does not mean overseen

This is the practical heart of the question, and it runs opposite to intuition.

People sometimes read legality as permission granted after scrutiny. In the unregulated category, nothing was scrutinised. No authority assessed the drug, set a protocol or inspected anyone.

It also means no authority is available afterwards. Where a medicine is regulated, there is a body to complain to and a record that accumulates.

New Zealand shows what that machinery looks like when it exists. A Health and Disability Commissioner decision examined the care of a patient who died at a clinic after receiving six ibogaine doses, in circumstances where the treating doctor had left the country and monitoring passed to an assistant [3].

That case is grim reading, and the existence of the decision is the point. A regulated setting produced an investigation, a published finding and consequences for the prescriber. An unregulated setting produces none of those, which does not mean such events do not occur there.

Gabon, where it is a tradition rather than a drug

One country belongs in a category of its own, and leaving it out would misrepresent the subject.

Iboga is native to west central Africa, and its use in Gabon long predates any pharmacology. There it is a ritual and cultural practice rather than a medical treatment, and the plant itself rather than an isolated compound is what is used.

Gabon regulates the export of iboga products under a cultural protection law rather than under drug control, which is a different legal logic from either prohibition or medicines regulation. The concern being addressed is the removal of a cultural and botanical resource.

That framing is worth holding for two reasons. It is a reminder that the substance has a history older than the research, and it marks a distinction between traditional preparations and the purified hydrochloride used in clinical settings, which differ in dose, consistency and what is known about them [4].

Why the history complicates the question

A claim circulates that ibogaine has never been a medicine anywhere. That is not accurate, and the history matters to how current law reads.

Ibogaine was isolated from iboga root bark in 1901 [4]. In France, tablets containing a small dose were sold for decades under the name Lambarene, marketed for fatigue, before being withdrawn in the 1960s.

So the substance has been sold as a product and is today a prescription medicine in several countries. What it lacks is a modern approval based on controlled trials, which is a different claim and the accurate one.

The historical picture also explains some of the legal variation. Countries that encountered ibogaine during the hallucinogen scheduling of the 1960s and 1970s tended to prohibit it, while those that met it later through medicines regulation tended to classify it.

Research happens under different rules

Studies run under research authorisations that are separate from the rules governing treatment.

The hospital study that produced the clearest cardiac data was conducted in a university psychiatry department in the Netherlands [6]. The published veterans research involved participants treated at a clinic in Mexico and assessed in the United States.

This has a consequence worth stating. The fact that research is permitted somewhere does not indicate that treatment is lawfully available there, and a provider citing research conducted in a country is not thereby operating under the conditions that research required.

What this means for travelling

Most people considering ibogaine live where it is prohibited or where no approved product exists, so travel is usually part of the question.

Several points follow, and they are practical rather than legal advice. Carrying ibogaine across a border is an offence in prohibited jurisdictions regardless of where it was obtained lawfully. Treatment abroad removes the aftercare question from the country where a person lives, and the published durability findings come from people who had arrangements afterwards.

Medical cover is the point most often overlooked. Travel insurance generally excludes treatment sought abroad for a condition, and may exclude complications arising from it.

There is also the matter of the journey home. The QT interval can remain prolonged after the drug has cleared [6], which makes the timing of a flight a medical question rather than a booking preference.

Status changes, and this page will age

Legal status is not stable, and several jurisdictions have moved in the past decade.

Anyone making a decision should verify current status directly, through the relevant medicines regulator or drug-control authority, rather than relying on any summary including this one. A provider's description of its own legal position is not verification.

Two checks are worth making. Whether the substance is controlled in the country of treatment, and separately whether the provider holds any medical licence that a patient could act on if something went wrong.

One further check is worth making before travelling, and it concerns money rather than law. In an unregulated setting there is usually no body that can compel a refund, investigate a complaint or require a provider to hold insurance. A dispute about a few thousand dollars and a dispute about clinical care are handled by the same absent authority.

The documentation question follows from it. Asking in advance for a written record of what will be given, in what doses, with what monitoring, and who will hold the clinical responsibility, is reasonable in any setting. In a regulated one those records exist by requirement. In an unregulated one they exist only if someone chooses to create them, and a provider unwilling to put the protocol in writing has answered the question.

Where the law goes next

Pressure for change comes mainly from the veterans research, which has attracted legislative interest in several places.

The 2026 scoping review frames the research priorities as standardised dosing, harmonised outcomes, long-term follow-up and product standardisation [4]. Those are also the things a regulator would require before approving a product.

Until that work exists, the legal position in most countries is likely to stay as it is, and the gap between where treatment is sought and where it is overseen will remain the defining feature. Frequently asked questions

Is ibogaine legal in the United States?

No. It is listed in Schedule I of the federal controlled substances schedules among hallucinogenic substances [1], and distribution is prosecuted [7].

Is ibogaine legal in New Zealand?

It is a prescription medicine there, classified in 2009 on the recommendation of Medsafe's classification committee [2]. No product is approved for marketing, so it is used as an unapproved medicine under the exemptions that allow a doctor to treat a patient in their care [3].

Does legal treatment mean safe treatment?

No. Legal status reflects a regulatory decision, not an assessment of a particular provider. A New Zealand case examined the death of a patient who received six doses in a setting where monitoring was delegated after the doctor left the country [3].

Can someone bring ibogaine home after treatment abroad?

Not where it is a controlled substance. In the United States it is a Schedule I substance [1], and carrying it across a border is an offence regardless of where it was obtained. Continuing treatment at home is a separate question from the session itself, and one worth settling before travelling.

Has ibogaine ever been sold as a medicine?

Yes. Tablets containing a small dose were sold in France for decades under the name Lambarene before being withdrawn in the 1960s [4]. What it lacks is a modern approval based on controlled trials.

Safety. Ibogaine prolongs the heart's QT interval and can trigger dangerous arrhythmias. Treatment requires an EKG and bloodwork before dosing, continuous cardiac monitoring during, and medically trained staff present. Ibogaine is not safe to take unsupervised.
