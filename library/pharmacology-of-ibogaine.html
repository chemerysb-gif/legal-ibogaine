The Pharmacology of Ibogaine: Targets, Kinetics, Metabolism

Ibogaine binds an unusually broad set of receptors at moderate affinity, then hands off to a long-lived metabolite. A technical but readable tour of what is known — and how much of it comes from animal data.

A promiscuous binding profile

Most modern CNS drugs are engineered for selectivity. Ibogaine, a natural product, is the opposite: it binds many targets at micromolar, moderate affinities rather than one target tightly. In drug development this is usually a defect. In addiction — a disorder distributed across opioid, monoamine, glutamate, and cholinergic systems simultaneously — polypharmacology may be precisely why the compound does what it does.

No single row of that table explains the clinical picture, and knocking out any one target in animal models attenuates rather than abolishes the effects — a pattern consistent with genuinely distributed action.

Kinetics and metabolism

Orally dosed ibogaine is absorbed and then demethylated by hepatic CYP2D6 into noribogaine. The parent compound clears within roughly a day; the metabolite binds tissue and clears over days to weeks. Two practical consequences follow. First, the clinical timeline has two phases — an acute 24-hour experience driven by ibogaine, and a long pharmacological tail driven by noribogaine (detailed in the noribogaine entry). Second, CYP2D6 activity varies genetically several-fold between individuals, so identical doses produce meaningfully different exposures — a core argument for individualized, medically supervised dosing and against fixed recipes.

The animal-to-human gap

A caution that applies to this entire entry: the mechanistic map is built largely on rodent pharmacology and in-vitro binding assays. Human receptor-occupancy imaging for ibogaine does not exist. The GDNF findings, the α3β4 story, and most dose-response curves are animal results whose human translation is assumed, not demonstrated. This is normal for a compound at this stage — but it means mechanistic confidence should lag clinical observation, not lead it.

Why the breadth may be the point

The emerging synthesis treats ibogaine less as "a psychedelic that treats addiction" and more as a multi-system reset: simultaneous modulation of the opioid system (withdrawal), monoamine systems (mood and craving), and plasticity pathways (relearning), followed by a weeks-long taper courtesy of its own metabolite. Whether that synthesis survives controlled trials is the field's central question — and it is exactly what engineered analogs are designed to dissect, target by target (see Beyond Ibogaine).
