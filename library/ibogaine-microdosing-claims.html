Ibogaine microdosing claims

Low-dose ibogaine is often presented as a gentler option. Single low doses were tolerated in volunteer studies, but repeated dosing was never tested and the cardiac effect has no demonstrated no-effect level.

Low-dose ibogaine is often presented as a way to obtain some benefit without the full experience and without the cardiac risk, taken repeatedly over weeks rather than once. The claim deserves a more careful answer than either enthusiasm or blanket dismissal, because there is relevant human data and it points in two directions at once.

Single low doses of ibogaine and of its metabolite were given to healthy volunteers in formal pharmacology studies and were tolerated. That is a real finding and it undercuts the suggestion that any amount is immediately dangerous. What those studies also show is that the cardiac effect appears at the lowest doses tested, that nobody has established a level below which it is absent, and that repeated dosing was never examined at all.

The claim

The usual version holds that a small fraction of a treatment dose, taken daily or several times weekly, produces mood or craving benefits while avoiding the psychoactive effects and the arrhythmia risk. Each element of that is a separate empirical claim. The first, about benefit, has no supporting data. The second, about the experience, is reasonable. The third, about cardiac safety, is where the evidence specifically contradicts the assumption.

A terminological point is worth settling first, because it causes real confusion. Microdosing in the psychedelic literature usually means a dose below the threshold of perceptible psychoactive effect, taken on a schedule. Applied to ibogaine, the term covers a wide range of practices, from a few milligrams daily to substantial fractions of a treatment dose taken weekly. Those are pharmacologically different situations, and a safety statement about one does not cover the other. Nothing below should be read as applying to a regimen it does not describe.

What the low-dose studies actually did

Two studies bear directly on this and both were conducted as formal clinical pharmacology. The first gave 36 healthy, drug-free men oral noribogaine at 3, 10, 30 or 60 milligrams, or placebo, in a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled ascending single-dose design, with sampling to 216 hours and pharmacodynamic tests chosen to detect opioid agonist effects [1].

Absorption was rapid with a peak at 2 to 3 hours, exposure was dose-linear from 3 to 60 milligrams, and elimination was slow, with mean half-life estimates of 28 to 49 hours across dose groups. The apparent volume of distribution was very high. No opioid agonist effects appeared on pupil size or cold-pressor testing, and no safety or tolerability issues were reported at these doses [1].

The second study pretreated 21 healthy volunteers with placebo or paroxetine for six days and then gave a single 20 milligram oral dose of ibogaine itself, far below treatment doses, with sampling over 168 hours. Single 20 milligram doses were safe and well tolerated [2].

Those results are worth stating clearly because they are usually either ignored or overstated. A single low dose of either compound did not produce safety problems in healthy screened volunteers. That is the strongest thing that can honestly be said for low-dose use, and the qualifications attached to it are what the rest of this entry concerns.

The noribogaine dose-ranging data

A third study is the one that bears on the cardiac question, and it used patients rather than healthy volunteers. Twenty-seven patients on methadone were switched to morphine and given noribogaine at 60, 120 or 180 milligrams in a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled single ascending dose design [3].

There was a concentration-dependent increase in the QT interval, with largest mean effects of about 16, 28 and 42 milliseconds at the three doses. Half-life was 24 to 30 hours. There was a non-significant trend toward lower withdrawal ratings [3].

The 60 milligram dose is the relevant number. It produced a mean QT increase of around 16 milliseconds, and 60 milligrams is the top of the range that the healthy-volunteer study described as producing no safety issues. Both statements are true: the dose was tolerated, and it measurably lengthened the QT interval. A measurable effect at a tolerated dose is not the same as no effect.

The populations in these studies also differ in a way that matters. The healthy-volunteer work used drug-free men screened for fitness, with no cardiac disease, no interacting medication and normal liver function [1] [2]. The patient study used people on opioid maintenance switched to morphine [3], which is closer to the population that would actually consider low-dose use. Safety findings from a screened healthy cohort are the most favourable case available, not a general result.

The absence of a no-effect threshold

The phrase concentration-dependent is the important one. The QT effect rose with concentration across the doses tested, which is the behaviour expected of a channel-blocking drug: the degree of block depends on how much drug is present at the channel. No study has identified a concentration at which the effect disappears.

That matters because the microdosing argument implicitly assumes a threshold exists, below which the cardiac effect switches off while some benefit persists. Nothing in the data supports such a threshold, and the underlying pharmacology does not predict one. Ibogaine blocks the hERG potassium channel, with half-maximal block reported at around 4 micromolar [5], and lower concentrations produce proportionally less block rather than none.

Less block is genuinely less risk, and a smaller QT change is safer than a larger one. The accurate framing is that low doses reduce the cardiac effect without eliminating it, which is a different proposition from the one usually offered.

The accumulation problem

This is the gap in the evidence that bears most directly on microdosing, because microdosing means repeated dosing and every study above used a single dose.

Noribogaine's mean half-life was 28 to 49 hours in healthy volunteers [1] and 24 to 30 hours in patients [3]. A compound with a half-life measured in days accumulates when dosed daily, reaching a steady-state concentration several times higher than the peak from a single dose. A regimen of small daily doses could therefore produce a sustained concentration comparable to one from a considerably larger single dose, and nobody has measured where that lands.

Two further features make accumulation harder to predict. Ibogaine is lipophilic, and the cardiac review notes storage in fat observed in rats [5], which creates a reservoir that releases slowly. And the QT prolongation outlasts the presence of ibogaine in the blood [5], so the cardiac effect does not resolve between doses as neatly as a plasma level might suggest.

Put together, repeated low dosing is the scenario in which the pharmacology is least characterised and the accumulation potential greatest. It is precisely what the volunteer studies did not test.

There is a monitoring consequence that rarely gets raised. The reason full-dose treatment involves an electrocardiogram and continuous telemetry is that QT prolongation is invisible without measurement. A person taking repeated low doses at home has no way of knowing what their QT interval is doing, and the effect they are relying on being absent is precisely the one they cannot observe. In that respect unsupervised low-dose use removes the monitoring while retaining a smaller version of the hazard it exists to detect.

No efficacy data at any low dose

The other half of the question is whether low doses do anything useful, and here the record is empty. The healthy-volunteer studies measured pharmacokinetics and safety, not benefit, and their participants were drug-free men with no condition to improve [1] [2]. The patient study reported a non-significant trend toward lower withdrawal ratings [3], which is a result that did not reach significance and was not what the study was designed to assess.

No study has examined low-dose or repeated-dose ibogaine for craving, mood, anxiety or substance use. A 2026 review of three decades of human research describes observational data in opioid use disorder, post-traumatic stress and polysubstance dependence [7], all of it from single large doses.

There is also no mechanistic reason to expect the single-dose findings to scale down. Whatever produces the reported durable effects is unresolved, with the 2019 review stating that the documented receptor interactions do not appear to account for ibogaine's long-lasting effects [6]. A mechanism that is not understood cannot be assumed to operate at a fraction of the dose.

Variation makes a small dose unpredictable

A further complication applies with particular force to small amounts. CYP2D6 genotype drove a more than tenfold spread in ibogaine clearance among patients, and QT prolongation tracked ibogaine concentrations rather than noribogaine concentrations [4].

A tenfold spread means a nominally small dose can produce a concentration an order of magnitude higher in one person than another. Someone designing a regimen around what they tolerated at a given amount has learned something about themselves and nothing transferable. For a person who clears the drug slowly, a repeated small dose is the combination most likely to accumulate.

The preparation question compounds this. Low-dose practice typically involves dividing material obtained without a certificate of analysis, and total alkaloid extract contains other iboga alkaloids in uncontrolled proportions. Calculating a small dose from material of unknown ibogaine content is an arithmetic exercise resting on an unverified assumption, and the smaller the intended dose, the larger the proportional error that a wrong assumption produces.

What it adds up to

The honest position has four parts. Single low doses of ibogaine and noribogaine were tolerated in healthy screened volunteers. The cardiac effect is present and concentration-dependent at those doses, with no identified level at which it vanishes. Repeated dosing has never been studied, and the long half-life makes accumulation the obvious concern. And no evidence of benefit exists at any low dose.

Low-dose use is therefore not the cautious middle path it is presented as. It is an entirely untested dosing regimen of a drug with a well documented effect on cardiac repolarisation, chosen on the unexamined assumption that a smaller amount must be proportionally safer and still useful, in a situation where the relevant pharmacology points toward accumulation rather than toward the proportional reduction the assumption requires.

Frequently asked questions

Is a low dose of ibogaine safe?

Single 20 milligram doses of ibogaine were safe and well tolerated in healthy volunteers [2], and single noribogaine doses to 60 milligrams produced no safety issues [1]. Repeated dosing was not tested, and at 60 milligrams noribogaine still raised the QT interval by about 16 milliseconds in patients [3].

Does a small dose avoid the heart risk?

It reduces it rather than removing it. The QT increase was concentration-dependent across all doses tested [3], and no concentration has been identified at which the effect is absent.

Has anyone studied low doses for a condition?

No. The low-dose work was clinical pharmacology in healthy volunteers measuring kinetics and safety, with no condition under treatment [1] [2], and the published observational evidence all concerns single large doses [7].

Why is repeated dosing the main concern?

Because of the half-life. Noribogaine's mean half-life was 28 to 49 hours [1], so daily dosing would accumulate toward a steady-state level considerably above a single-dose peak, and no study has measured that.

Do the healthy-volunteer safety findings apply generally?

They are the most favourable case rather than a general result. Those studies used drug-free men screened for cardiac disease, interacting medication and liver function [1] [2], and clearance varies more than tenfold on CYP2D6 genotype alone [4].

Does low-dose ibogaine help with craving or mood?

No study has examined it. The only relevant patient data reported a non-significant trend toward lower withdrawal ratings in a study designed to assess safety [3].

Safety. Ibogaine prolongs the heart's QT interval and can trigger dangerous arrhythmias. Treatment requires an EKG and bloodwork before dosing, continuous cardiac monitoring during, and medically trained staff present. Ibogaine is not safe to take unsupervised.
