Ibogaine clinical trials: what is registered

Registry records show more formal ibogaine research than the published literature reflects. Completed trials with no results posted, and the studies currently running.

Read only the published literature and ibogaine looks like a field of case series and observational cohorts with almost no formal trials. Read the clinical trial registries and a different picture appears: randomised, masked, placebo-controlled studies have been registered, run and completed. The reason they do not show up in the literature is that their results were never posted.

That gap is worth examining, both because it changes the apparent state of the field and because it is the kind of absence that affects what anyone can reasonably conclude. A completed trial whose results are withheld contributes nothing to knowledge while still having exposed participants to risk.

Why registry records matter

A trial registry entry is created before a study begins and records its design, its planned enrolment, its endpoints and its sponsor. Registration became standard practice precisely so that studies could not disappear when their results were unwelcome, since a registry entry stands as a public record that a trial existed regardless of what is subsequently published.

A registry record is not a results paper. It states what was intended and, in the fields that get updated, how many people were actually enrolled and whether the study completed. It does not say what was found, and it has not been peer reviewed. Treating a registry entry as evidence of efficacy would be a serious error; treating it as evidence that a study took place is exactly what it is for.

Registries also carry a specific limitation that matters here. Fields such as overall status and enrolment depend on the sponsor updating them, and a record last verified years ago may not reflect what has happened since. A study marked completed is reliable about having finished; one marked unknown means only that nobody has told the registry otherwise. Reading these records therefore involves distinguishing what is documented from what has merely stopped being maintained.

Spain: a masked phase 2 in methadone tapering

The most striking record is a phase 2 trial in Spain examining ibogaine for methadone detoxification, conducted with a university hospital in Reus and sponsored by a non-profit research foundation. It enrolled 20 adults on methadone maintenance, and its design was randomised, parallel and quadruple masked, meaning participant, care provider, investigator and outcomes assessor were all blinded [1].

The dosing arms are notable. One arm received six fixed doses of 100 milligrams of ibogaine hydrochloride; the other received ascending doses of 100, 200, 300, 400, 500 and 600 milligrams [1]. That is a quite different approach from the single large dose used in the case series, and a divided ascending regimen is a plausible strategy for limiting peak concentration and therefore cardiac risk.

The record shows the trial as completed. No results have been posted [1]. A quadruple-masked randomised trial is precisely the design the field has been said to lack, and its findings, whatever they were, are not available.

The United Kingdom: phase 1 and 2a

A second substantial record covers a phase 1 and 2a study of oral ibogaine in opioid withdrawal, run at two early-phase units in Manchester and London by a commercial developer. It enrolled 116 participants across both stages, with stage 1 in healthy volunteers and stage 2 in opioid-dependent adults seeking medically supervised withdrawal, who had to reach a defined withdrawal score before dosing [2].

Its design included a placebo-controlled stage assessing the effect on the QT interval, and a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled single-dose stage in opioid withdrawal [2]. A dedicated placebo-controlled QT assessment is the standard regulatory approach to characterising exactly the cardiac risk that dominates ibogaine safety discussion.

This study is also recorded as completed, with no results posted [2]. Taken together with the Spanish trial, that means two controlled studies totalling 136 enrolled participants, including a formal cardiac safety assessment, have produced no public findings.

Both trials are also worth noting for what their mere existence demonstrates about feasibility. A frequent argument holds that ibogaine cannot be tested conventionally because its effects are too obvious to blind and its cardiac risk too great to permit a placebo arm. Two completed studies, one quadruple masked and one with a placebo-controlled cardiac stage, show that ethics committees and regulators in Spain and the United Kingdom judged such designs acceptable and that they could be carried out. The obstacle to controlled evidence has not been that trials are impossible.

The missing results problem

The consequence is direct and it cuts in both directions. When a 2026 review of three decades of human research concludes that most positive efficacy data come from uncontrolled studies and that no double-blind randomised trial has shown ibogaine or noribogaine effective for opioid use disorder [7], that conclusion is drawn from what has been published. Controlled trials have been conducted. Their results are simply not in the literature to be reviewed.

It is a mistake to read unposted results as necessarily negative, and equally a mistake to assume they were positive and suppressed for other reasons. Trials go unreported for many reasons, including commercial decisions, loss of funding, recruitment problems and administrative failure. What can be said is that the information exists somewhere and that the people who took part contributed data which is doing no good.

For anyone weighing a decision, the practical implication is that the evidence base is thinner than the research effort. Studies capable of answering the central questions have run to completion, and the answers are unavailable.

An observational imaging study, recruiting

One study is currently active and takes an unusual approach. A prospective observational cohort at the University of California, Irvine plans to enrol around 20 adults with moderate to severe opioid use disorder who independently schedule treatment at a licensed clinic outside the United States. The university provides no treatment and does not fund it [3].

Participants undergo MRI, MR spectroscopy and EEG at three time points, with psychometric surveys and urine testing including follow-ups at three and six months. The study began in September 2025, is recruiting, and primary completion is estimated for May 2027 [3].

The design is a response to a legal constraint rather than a scientific preference. Ibogaine's United States scheduling makes administering it domestically impractical outside a federally approved protocol, so researchers observe people who obtain treatment elsewhere. It is the same arrangement that produced the Stanford veteran cohort, and it comes with the same limitation: no control group and no control over the treatment itself.

Trials of the analogs

The analog programmes have their own registry history, with a similar pattern. A phase 1 study of 18-MC in healthy volunteers, randomised, double-blind and placebo-controlled, with single-day and seven-day ascending cohorts, enrolled 108 participants in Perth, Australia. It completed in 2021 with no results posted [4].

A phase 2 trial of 18-MC was registered for cutaneous leishmaniasis, an indication unrelated to addiction, with 52 participants estimated. Its overall status is unknown, its last known status was not yet recruiting, the estimated start was May 2021 and it was last verified in March 2020 [5]. A 2025 review summarising the analog field states plainly that limited clinical trial results have been disclosed [6].

A claim circulates that 18-MC is in phase 2 trials for opioid use disorder. The registry records do not support it: the two registered 18-MC trials are the healthy-volunteer phase 1 and the leishmaniasis phase 2 [4] [5].

There is a second, quieter cost. Each unreported trial means the next team has to design its study without knowing what the last one found, which doses were tolerated, what the dropout pattern looked like or which endpoints turned out to be sensitive enough to detect a change. Participants in future trials consequently take on risks that earlier participants have already characterised, and funders pay to generate information that already exists. In a field this small, where two completed trials represent a meaningful share of all controlled work ever done, that duplication is expensive.

What is not being tested

The gaps are as informative as the entries. No registered trial examines ibogaine in a fentanyl-dependent population, which is now the dominant form of opioid dependence. No registered trial examines it for anxiety, depression or post-traumatic stress as a primary indication, despite those being where the largest reported effects have appeared. And no registered trial compares ibogaine against an active comparator such as buprenorphine or methadone, which is the comparison a clinician would need.

The one genuinely placebo-controlled randomised trial with published results in this family tested noribogaine rather than ibogaine, in 27 patients switched from methadone to morphine, and was designed as a safety study. It found a concentration-dependent rise in the QT interval, with mean effects of about 16, 28 and 42 milliseconds at the three doses, and a non-significant trend toward lower withdrawal ratings [8]. That is the controlled evidence base in its entirety.

Nor is there a registered trial examining the divided ascending dose approach that the Spanish study used, despite its obvious relevance to limiting peak concentration and therefore cardiac risk. Nothing addresses the preparation question either: no registered study compares purified ibogaine hydrochloride against the total alkaloid extracts that circulate in practice, so the material most people actually receive has not been formally tested at all.

How to read the position

Three statements are simultaneously true and are usually deployed against each other. Formal controlled research on ibogaine exists and has been completed. The published evidence for efficacy remains uncontrolled and at high risk of bias [7]. And the absence of published controlled results is a reporting failure rather than proof that the research was never attempted.

Anyone citing registry entries as evidence that ibogaine works is overreading them, since a registry says nothing about findings. Anyone citing the absence of controlled trials without acknowledging that completed ones exist unreported is describing the literature rather than the research. The accurate position is that the field has been studied more rigorously than its publication record shows, and that the results of that rigour are not available to anybody making a decision today.

Frequently asked questions

Have there been randomised controlled trials of ibogaine?

Yes, and their results are not published. A quadruple-masked phase 2 trial in Spain enrolling 20 patients and a phase 1 and 2a study in the United Kingdom enrolling 116 are both recorded as completed with no results posted [1] [2].

Does a registry entry mean a treatment works?

No. A registry records design, enrolment and status, not findings, and it is not peer reviewed. It establishes only that a study took place.

Is anything being studied now?

An observational imaging cohort at the University of California, Irvine is recruiting around 20 participants who arrange treatment abroad, with MRI, spectroscopy and EEG at three time points and primary completion estimated for May 2027 [3].

Why were the results never published?

The registries do not say. Trials go unreported for many reasons, including commercial decisions, lost funding, recruitment difficulties and administrative failure, and unposted results should not be read as evidence of a negative finding or of a positive one [1] [2].

Is 18-MC in trials for addiction?

Not according to the registries. The two registered 18-MC studies are a completed healthy-volunteer phase 1 with no results posted and a phase 2 in cutaneous leishmaniasis whose status is unknown [4] [5].

Safety. Ibogaine prolongs the heart's QT interval and can trigger dangerous arrhythmias. Treatment requires an EKG and bloodwork before dosing, continuous cardiac monitoring during, and medically trained staff present. Ibogaine is not safe to take unsupervised.
