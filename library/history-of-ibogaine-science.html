A Short History of Ibogaine Science, 1901–2026

From a French pharmacology lab to a Nature Medicine paper: the century-long, repeatedly interrupted scientific career of one alkaloid, in five periods.

Contents

  2. 1962–1970: the Lotsof observation
  3. 1970–1993: prohibition and hibernation
  4. 1993–2018: the observational era
  5. 2018–2026: institutionalization

1901–1960: isolation and obscurity

Ibogaine was isolated from iboga root bark around 1901 by French and Belgian chemists, decades after colonial botanists first described the plant — and centuries into its ceremonial use in the Bwiti tradition of Gabon, a context that long predates and outlasts every scientific period in this entry. Mid-century France briefly marketed a low-dose iboga extract as the tonic Lambarène; pharmacology papers appeared sporadically. Nothing suggested the compound's later significance.

1962–1970: the Lotsof observation

In 1962, Howard Lotsof, a 19-year-old heroin user in New York, took ibogaine for its psychoactive effects and noticed that his withdrawal never arrived and his craving was gone. He administered it informally to several dependent friends and observed the same. A single-digit, uncontrolled, retrospectively reported case series — and, unusually for such a thing, one whose central observation has been directionally reproduced by every structured study since. Lotsof spent the following decades as the compound's chief advocate, later patenting its use in dependence disorders.

1970–1993: prohibition and hibernation

The U.S. placed ibogaine in Schedule I in 1970 amid the general scheduling of psychedelics, and formal research effectively stopped for two decades. What survived was informal: self-treatment networks and activist "sessions" in the U.S. and the Netherlands, which generated the first systematic protocol knowledge — and, eventually, the case documentation that would define the compound's risk profile for clinical researchers to address.

1993–2018: the observational era

NIDA funded early protocol development in 1993, then dropped the program in 1995 — a decision that pushed ibogaine research offshore for a generation. The vacuum was filled by clinics in Mexico, the Caribbean, and elsewhere, and by academic groups studying their patients: Deborah Mash's St. Kitts series, structured follow-up studies in Mexico, and after New Zealand made ibogaine prescribable in 2010, the cleanest observational dataset in the literature. In parallel, the GDNF neuroplasticity findings gave the clinical observations their first serious mechanistic frame.

2018–2026: institutionalization

Three forces converged: the opioid crisis made unconventional candidates politically discussable, the broader psychedelic-medicine wave built regulatory scaffolding, and the 2024 Stanford MISTIC publication in Nature Medicine demonstrated that top-tier ibogaine research was possible. Texas followed in 2025 with $50 million in matched public trial funding. A century after isolation, the compound finally has what it never had: an institutional research pipeline. The century's lesson is less about the molecule than about how drug policy can freeze a scientific question for fifty years — and how quickly the question thaws when institutions decide to ask it.
