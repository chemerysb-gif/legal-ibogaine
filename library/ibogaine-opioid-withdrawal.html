What does ibogaine do to opioid withdrawal?

In three studies of people dependent on opioids, withdrawal scores fell sharply within about three days of a single supervised treatment. The relief came with a serious risk to the heart's rhythm, which is why screening and monitoring shape every part of treatment.

In three studies of people dependent on opioids, ibogaine eased withdrawal fast. After one treatment under medical care, symptoms dropped sharply within about three days [1][2][3]. In two of the studies, people's own withdrawal scores fell by about half. Ibogaine also carries a serious risk to the heart's rhythm. Treatment needs heart checks first and a close watch throughout [4].

Does ibogaine stop opioid withdrawal?

Ibogaine sharply reduced opioid withdrawal in three observational studies [1][2][3]. The change came fast. Each study saw the drop within about three days of a single treatment.

The studies took place in St Kitts, Mexico and New Zealand [1][2][3]. People came in using heroin, oxycodone, methadone and other opioids [2][3]. In the Mexico study, people had already been through an average of three earlier treatments for opioid dependence [2].

People weighing ibogaine usually want to know how much relief to expect and how soon it arrives. The evidence overview puts these studies in the wider picture.

In St Kitts and New Zealand, treatment took place under medical care, with health checks before anyone was dosed [1][3]. In St Kitts, heart monitoring ran from an hour before the dose to a full day after it [1].

How does ibogaine work in the brain?

Ibogaine acts on many targets in the brain at once [5]. It attaches to opioid receptors, to the pumps that recycle serotonin and dopamine, and to several other receptor types [5].

Most medicines act like one key cut for one lock. Ibogaine behaves more like a ring of keys. A 2019 research paper lists the locks it fits and describes the route to its effect on drug-seeking as unresolved [5]. The research brief on withdrawal interruption mechanisms goes deeper into the candidate pathways. How the body handles ibogaine is much clearer.

The body turns ibogaine into a second compound, noribogaine. An enzyme in the gut wall and liver, called CYP2D6, does the conversion [1]. Ibogaine itself leaves the blood quickly. Its half-life, the time the body takes to clear half of a dose, runs from about 1.6 to 6 hours [1].

Not everyone processes it at the same speed. Between 5 and 10 percent of people of European descent lack the gene for CYP2D6 [3]. In these slow processors, combined levels of ibogaine and noribogaine are projected to run about twice as high [3].

Noribogaine stays much longer. Given on its own to people coming off methadone, it had a half-life of 24 to 30 hours [6]. A single dose of ibogaine leaves this second compound circulating for days [1][6].

How much does ibogaine reduce opioid withdrawal, and how fast?

In two studies, people rated their own withdrawal. Their scores fell by about half within three days of one treatment [2][3]. In a third study, doctors scored withdrawal signs significantly lower 36 hours after the dose [1].

In Mexico, self-rated withdrawal fell from 31 points before treatment to 14 points about three days after it [2]. On this scale, higher scores mean worse withdrawal.

In New Zealand, where ibogaine is available on prescription, self-rated withdrawal fell from about 25 points to about 14 within 12 to 24 hours [3]. Most of that group were coming off methadone [3]. Methadone lasts a long time in the body, so its withdrawal drags on too [3].

One person enrolled in the New Zealand study died during treatment [3]. The coroner found the death very likely linked to ibogaine. The most probable cause was a heart rhythm problem [3].

St Kitts doctors scored the outward signs of withdrawal before and after dosing [1]. Thirty-six hours after the dose, those scores were significantly lower than at the start [1]. Opioid craving also fell on all five measures of a standard craving questionnaire by the end of the inpatient stay [1].

In a Dutch hospital study, people on methadone or buprenorphine switched to morphine, then took one ibogaine dose [4]. Most did not return to morphine in the next 24 hours [4]. The team rated withdrawal as mostly well tolerated and manageable [4].

In all three studies that scored it, withdrawal eased within about three days [1][2][3].

Nausea, vomiting and unsteady walking were the most common side effects in St Kitts [1]. About one person in twenty had a drop in blood pressure on standing up [1]. In the Dutch study, every patient had a spell of severe, temporary unsteadiness and could not walk without support [4].

Why does the relief last longer than ibogaine itself?

Ibogaine clears the blood within hours, while withdrawal relief in these studies held for days [1][2][3]. The researchers behind the St Kitts series point to noribogaine as the likely reason [1]. It is the longer-lasting compound the body makes from ibogaine. In rodent research, ibogaine's direct contact with receptors does not seem to explain its long-lasting effects [5].

On that view, the two work like runners in a relay. Ibogaine runs a short first leg. Noribogaine takes the baton and carries it much further.

Noribogaine is active in its own right. Given alone to people, it lengthens the heart's electrical recovery time, and the effect grows with the dose [6]. After an ibogaine dose, that lengthening lasted beyond 24 hours in close to half the patients in one study [4].

Animal research supports the withdrawal effect. In rodent studies, ibogaine reduces the withdrawal signs set off by drugs that block opioid receptors [5]. It also reduces how much morphine and heroin rodents choose to take [5].

How long does the relief from withdrawal last?

In the studies that followed people afterwards, improvement reached well beyond the first few days [1][2][3]. At one month, half the people treated in Mexico reported no opioid use in the previous 30 days [2].

Among people reached for follow-up in St Kitts, opioid craving was still lower at one month than before treatment [1].

In Mexico, drug-use scores were better than at the start at every check from one month to twelve months [2]. The biggest improvement came at one month [2]. Scores for legal and family problems also improved at every check [2].

In New Zealand, scores on a standard drug-use measure fell by more than 80 percent over twelve months [3]. Most of the group reported stopping or cutting down their opioid use [3]. At twelve months, three in four of those tested had urine free of opioids [3]. drinking less [3]. Depression scores fell from 22 to 4 on a standard scale over the same year [3]. The longer-term numbers are covered in clinical studies, summarized.

How the dose is given varies. In St Kitts and the Netherlands, people first switched from their usual opioid to short-acting morphine [1][4]. They then took a single dose, set by body weight [1][4]. In New Zealand, providers started with a small test dose. They added more over one to four days, guided by each person's response [3].

Dosing starts once withdrawal has begun. In St Kitts, withdrawal was scored about 12 hours after the last morphine, just before the dose [1]. In New Zealand, the last opioid came 12 to 33 hours before treatment, and the test dose waited until withdrawal was under way [3].

In St Kitts and New Zealand, the dose was one part of a longer process, with screening before it and supervision during it [1][3]. The weeks after treatment have their own guide: aftercare and integration.

What is ibogaine treatment like?

Ibogaine treatment usually moves through two stages. A vivid, dream-like stage comes first, then a calm, thoughtful one [1][7]. The first begins between about half an hour and three hours after the dose [1][7]. It lasts about 4 to 8 hours [1][7]. The second lasts roughly 8 to 20 hours [7].

In the first stage, people see a stream of images. Many of them are memories of past events [7]. In St Kitts, people treated for opioid or cocaine dependence often said it felt like a waking dream, or like watching a film [1]. Childhood memories came up for about four in ten [1]. More than half felt linked to a higher power, or to something larger than themselves [1].

Thinking felt different, and sounds could seem sharper [1]. In published accounts, hallucinations play a smaller part than the flood of remembered images [7]. In a Dutch hospital study, patients saw pictures behind closed eyes and relived vivid memories, and one reported hallucinations [4].

In the second stage, the images thin out. Attention turns to making sense of what came up, and the mood is mostly calm [1][7]. Some people still felt mild effects a day after the dose, and these soon faded [1]. Sleep is often hard for up to three days, and sleep medicine helps [7].

Looking back, most people found the experience worth it [1]. In St Kitts, nine in ten said it helped with their drug problem, and a similar share said it gave them insight [1]. Two in three felt they needed to stop using now [1]. Half said they felt cleansed or reborn, and four in ten felt they had a second chance at life [1]. About one in six said they would choose to go through it again [1].

In New Zealand, even people who kept using some opioids spoke well of the experience [3]. Many said it helped them understand their own situation [3].

Where the research goes next

Four directions are open. The first is a controlled trial against standard withdrawal care. Such a trial would show how much of the relief comes from ibogaine itself [1][2][3].

The second is follow-up that runs a full year and reaches everyone enrolled. New Zealand showed this can be done. Eight people there finished every interview across twelve months [3].

Third is heart monitoring built into the trial protocol. The Dutch team checked the heart at set times for at least a day. They gave magnesium once the QT interval passed 500 milliseconds [4]. Later trials have a working template to copy.

The fourth question is which compound does the work. Given on its own, noribogaine produced a trend toward lower withdrawal scores that was not statistically significant [6]. The relief may come from ibogaine, from noribogaine, or from both. The next round of research can settle it.

Frequently asked questions

How long does ibogaine take to work on withdrawal?

In the studies, withdrawal eased within about three days of treatment [1][2][3]. In New Zealand, self-rated withdrawal had already dropped within 12 to 24 hours [3]. In St Kitts, doctors recorded lower withdrawal signs at 36 hours [1].

Does ibogaine work for methadone withdrawal?

Most people in the New Zealand study were coming off methadone, and their withdrawal scores fell within a day of treatment [3]. In a Dutch study of people on methadone or buprenorphine, most did not return to morphine in the 24 hours after a dose [4]. Both groups were treated under medical supervision, with heart checks at set intervals in the Dutch study [3][4].

How long does ibogaine treatment take?

The dosing itself can take from one day to several. In New Zealand, providers gave doses over 24 to 96 hours, about 57 hours on average [3]. In a Dutch hospital study, heart monitoring ran for at least 24 hours after the dose [4].

How long does ibogaine stay in the body?

Ibogaine itself clears from the blood within hours, with a half-life of about 1.6 to 6 hours [1]. The body turns it into noribogaine, which has a half-life of 24 to 30 hours [6]. Effects on the heart can outlast the first day [4][6].

What does ibogaine do to the heart?

Ibogaine slows the heart's electrical reset between beats [4]. Doctors measure this as the QT interval. In a Dutch hospital study, the QT interval lengthened by about 95 milliseconds at its peak [4]. Heart rate also slowed [4].

Is ibogaine safe?

Ibogaine prolongs the heart's QT interval, which has been linked to sudden deaths [5]. In one hospital study, half the patients passed a QT of 500 milliseconds after a single dose [4]. Treatment requires screening and continuous heart monitoring, which lower the risk without removing it [1][4]; the safety protocols page describes what screening involves.

Safety. Ibogaine prolongs the heart's QT interval and can trigger dangerous arrhythmias. Treatment requires an EKG and bloodwork before dosing, continuous cardiac monitoring during, and medically trained staff present. Ibogaine is not safe to take unsupervised.
